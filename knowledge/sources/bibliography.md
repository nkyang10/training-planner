# Bibliography & source register

One line per source, prefixed by the owning research scope so parallel agents never collide:
`PHYS:` physiology & metrics · `PLAN:` periodization & plan construction · `FB:` feedback,
autoregulation, safety, heuristics · `PROD:` product/architecture research (this file is not written
by the research drop in `research/`, which keeps its own link list).

Format — the id in brackets is what `sources:` front matter points at:

```
- [PHYS-S1] Author (year). Title. Journal/Book. URL — grade A|B|C|D — what it supports
```

Grading (see [../MAINTENANCE.md](../MAINTENANCE.md#4-source-grading-in-sourcesbibliographymd)):
**A** meta-analysis/review/large cohort in runners · **B** RCT or cohort, small or non-runner ·
**C** consensus statement, coaching textbook, longitudinal coaching practice · **D** vendor
whitepaper, blog, single anecdote.

Grade-D sources may motivate a display, never a plan-mutating default.

### PHYS — heart-rate and threshold physiology

- [PHYS-S1] Tanaka H, Monahan KD, Seals DR (2001). "Age-predicted maximal heart rate revisited." *J Am Coll Cardiol* 37(1):153–156. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11153730/ — grade A — `HRmax = 208 − 0.7·age`; the default prediction equation and its population-level bias structure.
- [PHYS-S2] Kasiak E, et al. (2023). "Accuracy of maximal heart rate prediction equations in runners." *J Clin Med* 12(8):2884. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37109218/ — grade A — 13 equations × 4,043 recreational runners: RMSE **9.1–10.5 bpm**, 9 of 13 equations biased. The reason a predicted HRmax may never drive a ±5 bpm decision.
- [PHYS-S3] Gulati M, et al. (2010). "Heart rate response to exercise testing in apparently healthy women." *JAMA* 303(1):60–67. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20585008/ — grade B — `206 − 0.88·age` in women; evidence that a single mixed-sex HRmax equation mis-sets zones for part of the population.
- [PHYS-S4] Nes BM, Janszky I, Vatten LJ, Nilsen TI, Wisløff U (2013). "Age-predicted maximal heart rate in healthy subjects: the HUNT fitness study." *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 23(6):661–668. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22376273/ — grade B — `211 − 0.64·age`, **SEE 10.8 bpm**; large cohort, non-runner, used as the spread reference.
- [PHYS-S38] Jack Daniels. *Daniels' Running Formula* (3rd ed.), Human Kinetics — grade C — coaching-table distance→intensity ladder (marathon ≈80–88%, 10-k ≈95–98%, 5-k ≈97–100% vVO₂max) and %vVO₂max pace tables. Shape, not a constant. *(Same book is registered as [FB-S6] by the feedback agent; our citation is the pace tables.)*
- [PHYS-S39] Steve Magness (2020). *The Science of Running* (2nd ed.). *Record not abstract-verified on 2026-11-03.* https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=running+threshold+intensity+distribution+coaching+consensus — grade C — threshold fraction of vVO₂max and the "quality session every 48 h" coaching structure.

Impulse–response model of training effects (linear TRIMP). *Record not abstract-verified on 2026-11-03.* https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Banister+impulse+response+model+training+effects — grade B — origin of the dual (fitness/fatigue) response and of HR-weighted training impulse.
- [PHYS-S6] Morton RH, Fitz-Clarke JR, Banister EW (1990). - [PHYS-S7] Stagno GG, Thatcher R, van Someren KA (2007). - [PHYS-S8] Akubat I, Abt G (2011). - [PHYS-S9] Ulmer ZS, et al. (2019). "Test-retest reliability of metrics derived from GPS and heart-rate monitoring." *Biol Sport* 36(2):191–194. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31223197/ — grade B — session-load typical error **12.2%** (ice hockey); adopted as the generic weekly-load noise floor, labelled non-running.
- [PHYS-S10] Foster C, et al. (1996). "A new method for monitoring training intensity during sessions." *Wis Med J* 95(6):370–374. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8693756/ — grade B — origin of sRPE load (`CR-10 × min`); small origin study, widely replicated in team sport, weakly in running.
- [PHYS-S11] Foster C, et al. (2001). Monotony and strain. *Record not abstract-verified on 2026-11-03.* https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Foster+monotony+strain+training+illness — grade C — definitions of monotony (mean/SD) and strain; the ~1.5–2.0 flag is practitioner convention, not a validated cut-point.
- [PHYS-S29] Scheltinga G, et al. (2026). Perceptual vs physiological vs biomechanical load in recreational runners (open access). *Record located by database search; not abstract-verified beyond the divergence finding on 2026-11-03.* https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Scheltinga+perceptual+physiological+biomechanical+load+recreational+runners — grade B — within-session **divergence** of load families: do not average counters into one number.

### PHYS — acute:chronic workload and load-injury claims

- [PHYS-S12] Impellizzeri FM, Marston MR (2020). "Can the acute:chronic workload ratio predict injury? A review of proposed mechanisms and re-analysis of existing data." *Sports Med* (editorial/re-analysis). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32502973/ — grade A-and-negative — mathematical artefacts of ratio metrics (allometry, autocorrelation); the strongest available argument for **display-only** ACWR.
- [PHYS-S13] Maupin D, Schram B, Canetti E, Orr R (2020). "The Relationship Between Acute: Chronic Workload Ratios and Injury Risk in Sports: A Systematic Review." *Open Access J Sports Med* 11:51–75. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32158285/ (PMC7047972) — grade A (systematic review, **mostly team sport**) — 27 studies, Downs & Black quality 48.2–64.3%, interrater κ = 0.885; high variability across ratio definitions and reference groups; a **trend** toward lowest injury risk at **0.80–1.30**; EWMA possibly more sensitive than rolling averages; the authors state the method's issues "must be addressed before it is confidently used".
- [PHYS-S14] Dalen-Lorentsen T, Andersen TE, Bjørneboe J, Vagle M, Martin KN, Kleppen M, Fagerland MW, Clarsen B (2021). "A Cherry, Ripe for Picking: The Relationship Between the Acute-Chronic Workload Ratio and Health Problems." *J Orthop Sports Phys Ther* 51(4):162–173. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33472501/ — grade A (prospective cohort, **86 elite youth footballers**, not runners) — 105 days, 6,250 athlete-days, 196 health problems; **108 pre-specified analyses, only 23 (21%) significant**; the EWMA variant produced the largest share of "significant" findings (44%) — sensitivity and false-positive risk rise together.
- [PHYS-S15] Cloosterman L, et al. (2024). "Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio and Injury Risk in Recreational Runners: A Longitudinal Cohort Study." *J Athl Train*. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38291782/ (PMC11537214) — grade A — recreational runners, the closest population we have; flagged-session share by variant: weekly-change **33.4%** (95% CI 32.8–34.0), coupled RA **16.2%**, uncoupled RA 25.8%, EWMA 18.9%; **43%** of weekly-change flags disagree with the ratio variants.
- [PHYS-S16] Gabbett TJ (2016). "The training—injury prevention paradox: should athletes be training smarter and harder?" *Br J Sports Med* (narrative review). *Record found, abstract not re-read on 2026-11-03.* https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26758673/ — grade B — the original underdog/high-training-load hypothesis; hypothesis-generating, not a threshold.
- [PHYS-S17] Gabbett TJ (2017). "Debunking the myths about training load, injury and performance: empirical, methodological and statistical considerations." *Br J Sports Med* (review). *Record found, abstract not re-read on 2026-11-03.* https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Gabbett+debunking+the+myths+about+training+load — grade B/C — coach-facing framing of the sweet spot; the source of the popular 0.8–1.3 band that the critics call circular.
- [PHYS-S49] Waddington G, Rabbat A (2017). Editorial on the training-injury prevention paradox. *Br J Sports Med*. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28442129/ — grade A (editorial) — critical position: the paradox should not drive practice before causal testing.
- [PHYS-S50] Zouhal H, et al. (2021). "Interpreting the acute:chronic workload ratio in athletes: a critical review." (editorial/review). https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8138569/ — grade B/C — the methodological critique camp; supports `series_version` and variant-labelled display.

### PHYS — HRV, resting HR and readiness signals

- [PHYS-S18] Plews DJ, Laursen PB, Stanley J, Kilding AE, Buchheit M (2013, October). "Training adaptation and heart-rate variability in elite endurance athletes." *Sports Med* 43(10):973–981. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23852425/ — grade B — HRV **log-ratio** interpretation, the ~7-day moving average, and the direction rules (higher vagal activity with fatigue vs performance gain).
- [PHYS-S19] Bellenger CR, Fuller JT, Thomson RO, Buckley JD (2016). "The metric of post-exercise heart rate recovery to monitor training load." *Sports Med* (systematic review/meta). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26888648/ — grade A — standardised mean differences for HR-derived markers vs performance direction: resting RMSSD **0.58** ↑, HF **0.55** ↑, post-exercise RMSSD **0.60** ↑, HRR **0.63** ↑; **non-specific**: resting RMSSD **0.26** ↓, post-exercise RMSSD **0.64** ↓, HR acceleration **−0.48**. Direction, never magnitude.
- [PHYS-S20] Altini M, Plews DJ (2021). "What Is behind Changes in Resting Heart Rate and Heart Rate Variability? A Large-Scale Analysis of Longitudinal Measurements Acquired in Free-Living." *Sensors (Basel)* 21(23):7932. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34883936/ — grade B — **28,175 users / ~9 million nights**: non-training confounders (alcohol, illness, heat, sleep loss, menstrual cycle) dominate single-night HRV/RHR; supports individual baselines and confound flags.
- [PHYS-S21] Dobbs WC, Fedewa MV, MacDonald HV, Holmes CJ, Cicone ZS, Plews DJ, Esco MR (2019). "The Accuracy of Acquiring Heart Rate Variability from Portable Devices: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis." *Sports Med* 49(3):417–435. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30706234/ — grade A — portable-device HRV accuracy is device- and setting-dependent, short recordings inflate error; the reason `#min_valid_night_pct`, the artefact gate and the "no recovery percentage" rule exist.

### PHYS — detraining, adaptation timing and intensity distribution

- [PHYS-S22] Zheng J, Pan T, Jiang Y, Shen Y (2022). "Effects of Short- and Long-Term Detraining on Maximal Oxygen Uptake in Athletes: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis." *Biomed Res Int* 2022:2130993. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36017396/ (PMC9398774) — grade A (athletes; **V̇O₂max only**) — effect size **−0.62** (short detraining) vs **−1.42** (long); no additional loss between 30–90 d and >90 d (Q = 0.54, p = 0.46). ### PLAN — intensity distribution and periodization models

- [PLAN-S1] Seiler KS, Kjerland GØ (2006). "Quantifying training intensity distribution in elite endurance athletes: is there evidence for optimal distribution?" *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 16(1):49–56. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16430681/ (DOI 10.1111/j.1600-0838.2004.00418.x) — grade **B** — **n = 11 junior male cross-country skiers** (not runners), 32 days, 384 sessions (347 endurance): time in 3 HR zones **75 ± 3% / 8 ± 3% / 17 ± 4%**; sRPE 76 ± 4 / 6 ± 5 / 18 ± 7%; blood-lactate distribution (n = 60 sessions) 71% / 7% / 22%. Supports: the observed pyramidal shape and the "small moderate zone, non-trivial high zone" pattern behind `intensity-distribution.yaml#pct_high_zone`. The population caveat must travel with the numbers.
- [PLAN-S2] Stöggl T, Sperlich B (2015). "Polarized training: has the international consensus view on endurance training been oversimplified?" *Front Physiol* 6:295. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26578968/ (DOI 10.3389/fphys.2015.00295) — grade **A** (narrative review) — reviewed distributions are **mostly pyramidal**, some world-class athletes polarized within specific phases; prospective comparisons favour polarized over threshold-heavy (HVLIT-heavy) designs; findings are training-level- and block-length-dependent. Supports: the model table in `periodization/models-comparison.md`, the "polarized ≈ pyramidal overall, optimum contested" stance, and the rule that a distribution default may never be a fake 80/20 constant.
- [PLAN-S3] Rosenblat MA, Perrotta AS, Vicenzino B (2019). "Polarized vs. pyramidal intensity training distribution's effect on endurance performance: A systematic review and meta-analysis." *J Strength Cond Res*. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29863593/ (DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000002618) — grade **A** (meta-analysis of small studies) — pooled advantage for the more polarized/pyramidal grouping over threshold-heavy, **ES −0.66 (95% CI −1.17 to −0.15)**. Supports: "threshold-heavy is the distribution that reliably fails", i.e. the ceiling on `pct_moderate_zone` — a direction and a range, not a target effect.
- [PLAN-S4] Esteve-Lanao J, Foster C, Seiler KS, Lucia A (2007). "Impact of interaction intensity distribution on body composition, performance, and aerobic capacity in competitive, professional runners." *J Strength Cond Res* 21(3):943–949. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17685689/ (DOI 10.1519/R-19725.1) — grade **B** controlled comparison, **n = 12 competitive runners**, 10 weeks — pyramidal vs near-threshold-heavy arms: **80.5 ± 1.8 / 11.8 ± 2.0 / 8.3 ± 0.7%** vs **66.8 ± 1.1 / 24.7 ± 1.5 / 8.5 ± 1.0%** in Z1/Z2/Z3; 10-km time change **−157 ± 13 s vs −121.5 ± 7.1 s (p = 0.03)**. Supports: the runners-specific distribution evidence, the junk-mile rule (an easy session within ~10 s/km of half pace is a generator bug), and the upper bound on `pct_moderate_zone`. Small n ⇒ a range, never a constant.
- [PLAN-S6] Rosenblat MA, Perrotta AS, Thomas SG, et al. (2025). "Intensity distribution of training in endurance athletes: A systematic review and meta-analysis of individual participant data." *Sports Med* 55(3):655–673. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39888556/ (DOI 10.1007/s40279-024-02149-3) — grade **A** (individual-participant-data network meta-analysis) — record located and registered, but **its abstract numbers were not captured in this pass, so no figure from it is quoted anywhere in `knowledge/`.** Supports: the existence of a newer, higher-quality synthesis that may supersede S1–S4; cite it only as "contested, pending verification", `confidence: low`.

### PLAN — taper, load progression and training-error evidence

- [PLAN-S5] Bosquet L, Montpetit J, Arvisais D, Mujika I (2007). "Effects of tapering on performance: a meta-analysis." *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 39(1):135–142. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17762369/ (DOI 10.1249/mss.0b013e31806010e0) — grade **A** meta-analysis — the verbatim conclusion quoted in `plan-construction/taper-and-peak.md`: "A 2-wk taper during which training volume is exponentially reduced by 41-60% seems to be the most efficient strategy to maximize performance gains." Supports: `taper.yaml#taper_days`, `#volume_cut_pct` (41–60), the exponential shape, and the cut-order "volume first, intensity preserved, frequency last".
- [PLAN-S7] Nielsen RO, Bonde AD, Sørensen H, Lindstrup SE, Sørensen CG, Thrane I, Thomsen L, Brunner-Jensen B, Christensen R, Lohmann ML, Buist I (2012). "Training errors and running related injuries: a systematic review." *Int J Sports Phys Ther* 7(1):58–75. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22389869/ — grade **A** (systematic review) — **abstract not read in this pass**: only the title-level framing is used (training errors as the most-studied modifiable risk factor for running injuries; rapid rises in volume/intensity/terrain and long-run jumps as the named error types), at `confidence: medium`, with effect estimates explicitly not quoted.
